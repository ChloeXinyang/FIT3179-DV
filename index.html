FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · Australian Census: People and Communities

One Country,
Many Communities

National averages tell only part of Australia’s story. Local communities often look quite different.

This page is designed for a general Australian audience. My aim is to show what national averages hide about everyday life across Australia: where age profiles differ, how education and work vary across the life course, and how income changes with place and household structure. The 2021 Census provides the main comparison. A separate June 2026 JobSeeker dataset adds a later snapshot, but it measures something different and is not treated as a direct update of Census unemployment.

01 · Start wide

Population is concentrated, but age profiles still differ

The population is concentrated in a few large states, but the age mix is not the same everywhere. I start with the broad national pattern, then zoom down to local communities.

The biggest states dominate the population, but each contains a different age mix

The nested treemap compares population size and age structure at the same time. NSW accounts for 31.8% of the population, Victoria 25.6% and Queensland 20.3%; together they make up 77.6% of residents across the eight main states and territories. Inside each state, the smaller rectangles show how that population is split across broad age groups rather than treating population size as the whole story.

Takeaway NSW, Victoria and Queensland take most of the space. Inside each state, 25–44 is usually one of the largest age blocks.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021 Census General Community Profile (G04). ABS Census DataPacks ↗

The national total hides large local differences in median age

State averages can hide large differences between nearby communities, so this map moves down to SA2 level. Each polygon is coloured by median age, while hover reveals the SA2 name, state, median age and resident population. The point is not to rank states, but to show that communities within the same state can have quite different age profiles.

Hover the map: the current SA2 is outlined and its name, state, median age and population appear in the tooltip.

Grey areas have no valid resident median-age value.

Source: ABS 2021 Census G02 and ASGS Edition 3 geography. Census DataPacks ↗ · ASGS geography ↗

The population pyramid shows where the national age structure is widest

The population pyramid compares males and females on the same age scale. The 30–34 group is the largest overall, with about 1.85 million people (948,896 females and 904,450 males). At 85+, the gap reverses strongly: about 331,000 females compared with 210,000 males.

On-chart annotation: 30–34 is the largest age group. At older ages, the female side becomes visibly wider.

Male values are plotted as negative only to create the pyramid; tooltip counts remain positive.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile (G04). ABS Census DataPacks ↗

The same five age groups form very different state profiles

Using percentages instead of raw counts makes small and large jurisdictions directly comparable. People aged 25–44 make up 33.8% of the Northern Territory and 32.4% of the ACT, but only 25.6% of Tasmania. For the 65+ group, Tasmania reaches 20.9%, compared with just 9.0% in the Northern Territory.

Compare NT and ACT have the longest 25–44 wedges, while Tasmania and South Australia stand out for larger 65+ shares.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

Population tells us where people are. Age starts to explain why places can feel different. The next step is to follow the life course from qualifications into work.
02 · Life course

Education and work look different across the life course

Qualifications are not distributed evenly across generations, and employment changes sharply from the teenage years to retirement.

Qualification profiles shift across age groups

This heatmap compares qualification profiles within each age group rather than comparing population totals. Bachelor degrees are most prominent at ages 25–34 (34.9%) and 35–44 (31.8%), but fall to 12.3% at ages 85+. Certificate III/IV is especially visible for ages 15–24 (31.4%) and 55–64 (29.1%).

Outlined cells: Bachelor peaks at 34.9% for ages 25–34; Certificate III/IV is 31.4% for ages 15–24.

Percentages use the age-group totals in Census table G49. “Other / not stated” is retained because it becomes important at older ages.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Table G49. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

Work participation changes even more clearly

The 100% stacked bars show how the employment mix changes across the life course. Full-time employment is 48.3% for ages 25–34 and just over 50% for ages 35–54. By ages 65–74, 73.1% are outside the labour force. Among 15–19-year-olds, part-time work is much more prominent at 29.5%.

Turning point Full-time work is about half of the 25–54 groups. By 65–74, 73.1% are outside the labour force.

“Unemployed” and “Not in the labour force” are different Census categories.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Table G46. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

Age explains part of the education and work pattern. But those patterns are also geographical. The next section asks whether places with more degree-qualified residents also tend to have higher household incomes.
03 · Place & opportunity

Education and income are linked, but local outcomes still vary

I first map the combination, then strip the map away and look directly at the relationship between the two variables.

Some communities combine high education and high household income

This bivariate map combines Bachelor+ attainment and median weekly household income for 2,402 SA2s. There are 533 SA2s in the high-education/high-income combination and 558 in low-education/low-income, but the mixed groups matter too: for example, 242 SA2s are high-education/middle-income. The map therefore shows where the two measures align and where they do not.

The relationship is easier to see without colouring every state

Each dot represents one of 2,331 SA2s with at least 500 residents. Across these areas, Bachelor+ share and median weekly household income have a positive correlation of about r = 0.68. The median SA2 sits at roughly 21.4% Bachelor+ and $1,755 per week, but the wide spread around the trend line shows that education does not explain every local income difference.

Optional filter: use the state / territory selector above the plot to focus the dots. The black national trend line stays fixed for comparison.
Main pattern The relationship is positive overall (r ≈ 0.68), but the wide spread shows that education is not the only factor linked with household income.

Dashed lines mark the median SA2 values. The solid line is a simple overall linear trend. This is an area-level association, not a causal claim.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Tables G02 and G43. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

State averages still hide a lot of variation inside each state

The ridgelines compare the distribution of SA2 median household incomes rather than reducing each jurisdiction to one average. The median of SA2 medians is about $2,416/week in the ACT, $2,038 in the NT, $1,818 in Victoria and $1,332 in Tasmania. The width of each distribution also shows that communities within the same jurisdiction can still differ substantially.

Example ACT sits further to the right overall, while Tasmania sits further left, but both still show a range of local community incomes.

Each SA2 contributes one observation. This is a distribution of community medians, not individual household incomes.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Table G02. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

Income is not only about location. Household structure changes the picture again, because one-person, couple and larger family households are very different units.
04 · Home life

Household structure changes how income should be read

Household size is strongly linked with household type, and family and non-family households sit in very different income bands.

Household size flows into very different household types

The alluvial view follows household size on the left into family or non-family household type on the right. One- and two-person households together account for about 59.1% of households. One-person households are entirely non-family, but 91.8% of two-person households are family households; by four persons the family share rises to 98.2%.

Read the flow One-person households move entirely to non-family. From two persons onward, the family stream quickly becomes dominant; 91.8% of two-person households are family households.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Table G35. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

A dumbbell chart makes the household income gap easier to compare

The dumbbell chart compares family and non-family households within the same weekly income bands. The largest difference is below $800/week: 47.1% of non-family households are in this band, compared with 12.3% of family households. At $3,500+, the pattern reverses, with 23.3% of family households versus 4.2% of non-family households.

Family household Non-family household

The labels on the chart pick out the two clearest contrasts: low-income non-family households and high-income family households.

The two household types are normalised separately. “Income partly/not stated” is excluded from the plotted percentages.

Source: ABS 2021 Census General Community Profile, Table G33. ABS Census DataPacks ↗

The Census is detailed, but it is a 2021 snapshot. To finish the story, I add one later administrative dataset from 2026.
05 · Later snapshot

A later JobSeeker snapshot adds context, not a direct comparison

The final map is not a replacement for the Census. It simply shows where larger JobSeeker Payment recipient counts were recorded in June 2026.

Where are larger JobSeeker recipient counts recorded?

Each circle shows the number of JobSeeker Payment recipients recorded for an SA2 in June 2026, so circle area represents a count rather than a rate. The largest counts in this dataset include East Arnhem (2,395), Dandenong - North (2,210) and Whyalla (2,085). These values show where larger recipient counts were recorded, not where the likelihood of receiving JobSeeker was highest.

Important Bigger circles do not mean a higher JobSeeker rate. Large-population SA2s can also produce large counts.

Symbols use SA2 representative points, not exact recipient locations. DSS JobSeeker counts are not the same concept as the 2021 Census unemployment measure.

Source: Australian Government Department of Social Services, Benefit and Payment Recipient Demographics, June 2026; ABS ASGS Edition 3 geography. DSS dataset ↗ · ASGS geography ↗

What should an Australian reader take away?

The main finding is that a national average is not a typical Australian community. Population is concentrated in NSW, Victoria and Queensland, but local age profiles still vary. Qualifications and labour-force participation look different across age groups. At SA2 level, higher Bachelor+ shares are generally associated with higher household incomes, although the relationship is not uniform. Household composition adds another layer: family and non-family households occupy very different income ranges.

The final 2026 JobSeeker map is a separate count snapshot, not evidence that unemployment has risen or fallen since 2021. For a general audience, the practical point is simple: place, age and household structure all matter before a national number can be interpreted as “typical”.
